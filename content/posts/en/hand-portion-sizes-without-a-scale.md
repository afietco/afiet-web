*Written by Afi, afiet's mascot · Last updated: 6 August 2026*

A portion of protein is roughly a **palm**. A portion of vegetables is a **fist**. A portion of grains or starch is a **cupped hand**. A portion of added fat is a **thumb**. That is the entire system. It needs no scale, no packet labels and no app, it works in a restaurant and at someone else's dinner table, and because the measure is your own hand it already scales to your body. Below is what each measure means in grams, how to apply it to real meals, and where it stops being reliable. This is general guidance, not personalised dietary advice.

## Why a hand instead of a gram

A gram is a fixed number sitting in a kitchen. Your hand belongs to you, and a bigger hand usually belongs to a body that needs more food, so the measure adjusts itself without you doing anything.

The practical argument is stronger still. You always have your hands with you. They need no charging, they work in a canteen at 13:00 with colleagues waiting, and nobody notices you using them.

> The best measuring tool is the one that is still with you in six months.

National guidance leans on the same instinct. The NHS defines an adult portion of fruit or vegetables as [80 g](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/portion-sizes/), then immediately explains it for children as "the amount they can fit in the palm of their hand", because that is what people can actually use.

## The four measures, with the numbers behind them

| Hand | Group | Women | Men |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Palm | Protein foods | ~85 g cooked | ~115 g cooked |
| Fist | Vegetables | ~1 cup | ~1 cup |
| Cupped hand | Grains, starch | ~100 g cooked | ~130 g cooked |
| Thumb | Added fat | ~14 g | ~14 g |

These figures come from [Precision Nutrition's hand portion guide](https://www.precisionnutrition.com/hand-portion-faq), which sets a palm at "~3 oz (85 g) cooked meat / tofu" for women and "~4 oz (115 g)" for men, a cupped hand at "~½ cup (100 g) cooked grains / legumes" and "~⅔ cup (130 g)" respectively, and a thumb at "~1 tablespoon (14 g) oils, nuts, seeds, nut butters".

The British Dietetic Association arrives in the same place from the other direction, describing a [140 g portion of cooked fish as "palm of hand"](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/food-facts-portion-sizes.html). Two independent sources, one hand.

A starting frame for a meal, again from Precision Nutrition: "1-2 palms of protein-dense foods at each meal", "1-2 fists of vegetables at each meal", "1-2 cupped handfuls of carbohydrates at most meals" and "1-2 thumbs of fat-dense foods at most meals".

## How to hold your hand properly

Small details change the reading, so it is worth being precise once:

1. **Palm.** The flat part only, fingers not included. Thickness counts too: a palm of chicken is as thick as your palm, not a thin slice covering the same area.
2. **Fist.** A closed fist, and for vegetables you can be generous. Very few people overeat salad.
3. **Cupped hand.** One hand, cupped, filled level rather than heaped. This is the measure people most often stretch.
4. **Thumb.** The whole thumb, tip to base. It is for fats you add: oil, butter, tahini, nut butter.

## Real meals, read in hands

The system only becomes useful when you can apply it to food you actually eat.

**Eggs with tomatoes and cheese.** Two eggs are roughly a palm of protein. The tomatoes and peppers are a fist of vegetables. The cheese adds a little more protein and fat, and the bread alongside is a cupped hand. That plate is already balanced.

**Lentil soup with bread and salad.** A bowl of lentil soup is protein and starch together, call it a palm plus most of a cupped hand. The salad is a fist. A slice of bread finishes the cupped hand. Add a spoon of olive oil on the salad and there is your thumb.

**Grilled chicken with rice and salad.** The classic. A palm of chicken, a cupped hand of rice, one or two fists of salad, a thumb of olive oil.

**Pasta.** This is where hands earn their keep. A restaurant plate of pasta is often three or four cupped hands with no protein and no vegetables anywhere. You do not need a calculator to see the shape is off; you need a fist of something green next to it and a palm of something protein-ish in it.

## Where the hand method stops working

Honesty matters more than tidiness, so here are its limits.

It is an estimate, not a measurement. It will land you in the right neighbourhood, not on a precise number, and that is the deal you accept in exchange for a method you can keep.

It reads mixed dishes poorly. A moussaka or a stew is protein, vegetables, starch and fat cooked into each other. The workable move is to estimate the main components rather than the dish, or to let a food catalogue do it for you.

It says nothing about quality. Four cupped hands of white bread and four of bulgur read identically to your hand and behave differently in your body. Hands measure amount; the food groups handle the rest, which is why the two go together. The full picture is in [what is a balanced plate](/en/blog/what-is-a-balanced-plate).

And it does not apply to children in the same way. Their portions scale with age and growth, and that is a conversation for a paediatrician rather than a blog.
