You can eat well without counting a single calorie. Instead of adding numbers, you look at what is on the plate: some protein, plenty of vegetables, a sensible amount of grains, a little fat, and something from the dairy and fruit groups across the day. Your hand does the measuring, your eyes do the checking, and the day balances out over lunch and dinner rather than at every single bite. This guide explains how that works in practice, what to do on the days it does not, and where the approach comes from. Nothing here is medical or personalised dietary advice.

## Why counting is not the same as eating well

Calorie counting is a measurement system, not a diet. It can tell you how much energy a meal carries, and it says nothing about whether the meal had any vegetables in it. You can hit a number precisely on a day made of biscuits and coffee, and miss it on a day of lentil soup, salad and yogurt.

There is a second problem, and it is the practical one. Counting asks you to weigh, look up, log and remember, three times a day, forever. Most people can do that for a few weeks. Almost nobody does it for a few years. A method you cannot keep is not a method; it is a temporary state you eventually fall out of, usually with a side order of guilt.

> A day is not a spreadsheet. It is four or five moments where you decide what to put on a plate.

## The alternative: balance you can see

The approach afiet uses, and the one most national food guides describe, is proportional rather than numerical. You look at the shape of the plate and the spread across the day.

The World Health Organization puts the vegetable side plainly: [everyone older than 10 years of age should aim for **at least 400 grams of fruits and vegetables per day**](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet). The UK's NHS Eatwell Guide describes the same thing as proportions of what you eat, with fruit and vegetables and starchy foods each making up "just over a third of the food we eat each day".

Then comes the sentence that takes most of the pressure off, straight from the [NHS Eatwell Guide](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/the-eatwell-guide/):

> "You do not need to achieve this balance with every meal, but try to get the balance right over a day or even a week."

That single line is the difference between a rule and a rhythm. A breakfast of bread and cheese is not a failure. It is one part of a day that still has lunch and dinner in it.

## Your hand is the measuring tool

If you are not weighing food, you need some way to judge amounts. The most practical one is already attached to you. Four hand shapes cover the four things worth judging:

| Hand | What it measures | Everyday examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Palm | Protein foods | Chicken, fish, eggs, cheese, lentils |
| Fist | Vegetables | Salad, cooked greens, tomatoes, peppers |
| Cupped hand | Grains and starch | Rice, pasta, bulgur, potato, bread |
| Thumb | Added fat | Olive oil, butter, tahini, nut butter |

This is not a folk method. Precision Nutrition, which popularised the system, puts a palm of protein at [about 85 g cooked meat for women and 115 g for men](https://www.precisionnutrition.com/hand-portion-faq), and suggests "1-2 palms of protein-dense foods at each meal" alongside "1-2 fists of vegetables at each meal". The British Dietetic Association describes [a 140 g portion of cooked fish as "palm of hand"](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/food-facts-portion-sizes.html). The numbers behind the hand are real; you simply do not have to touch them.

The quiet advantage is that a hand scales with the body it belongs to. A larger person tends to have a larger palm, and tends to need more food. The measure adjusts itself, which no gram figure on a packet can do.

We wrote a full walkthrough with real meals in [hand portion sizes: how to measure your plate without a scale](/en/blog/hand-portion-sizes-without-a-scale), and you can get your own day's worth in the [daily portions calculator](/en/tools/daily-portions-calculator).

## What a day looks like in practice

Balance is easier to keep when you stop thinking meal by meal and start thinking day by day. A realistic shape:

1. **Breakfast.** Something with protein in it, and at least one vegetable or fruit. Eggs with tomatoes and cucumber. Yogurt with fruit. Cheese with bread and olives, plus something green next to it.
2. **Lunch.** A palm of protein, one or two fists of vegetables, a cupped hand of grains. A bowl of lentil soup with salad and a slice of bread does all three.
3. **Dinner.** The same shape, with whatever is missing from the day. If lunch had no vegetables, dinner is where they go.
4. **In between.** Fruit, nuts, yogurt. Nothing needs to be earned.

Notice what is absent: no forbidden foods, no meals to skip, no numbers to hit. Cake at a birthday is part of the day, not a hole in it.

## The five groups, and why colour beats arithmetic

Balance is easier to track by group than by nutrient. Five groups carry most of what a day needs: vegetables, fruit, protein foods, grains, and dairy. When afiet shows you a day, it shows those five as colours filling in, not as a running total. You can see at a glance that the protein circle is full and the vegetable one is barely started, which is exactly the information that changes what you cook tonight.

Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate gives the same picture in proportions: ["make most of your meal vegetables and fruits, ½ of your plate"](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-eating-plate/), a quarter whole grains, a quarter protein. If you want the longer version, read [what is a balanced plate](/en/blog/what-is-a-balanced-plate).

## What to do on the days it falls apart

Some days are bread and coffee at a desk. This is where most eating plans quietly end, because the plan has no language for an imperfect day other than failure.

The honest answer is that one unbalanced day changes very little. Bodies work on averages over weeks, which is precisely why the NHS talks about a day or even a week rather than a meal. The useful move after a scattered day is not compensation, and definitely not skipping the next meal. It is simply the next plate: put a fist of vegetables and a palm of protein on it and carry on.

## Where the food comes from matters less than you think

Balanced eating is not a cuisine. It is a set of proportions, and almost every food culture can meet them with its own dishes. afiet grew up around the Turkish table, where lentil soup, olive-oil vegetables, yogurt and bulgur are ordinary weekday food and already sit close to those proportions. The parts that need attention there are bread, salt and dessert, not the whole way of eating. We looked at that honestly in [is Turkish food healthy?](/en/blog/is-turkish-food-healthy).

Your own table will have its own strengths and its own two or three things worth watching. The method does not change.

## Start with one plate

If you take one thing from this: stop trying to fix the whole week tonight. Look at the plate in front of you and ask a single question, whether there is a vegetable on it. That question, asked often enough, does most of the work that a counting app promises and rarely delivers.
