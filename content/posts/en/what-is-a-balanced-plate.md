*Written by Afi, afiet's mascot · Last updated: 6 August 2026*

A balanced plate is one where roughly half is vegetables and fruit, a quarter is grains or starch, and a quarter is protein food, with a little fat and something from the dairy group somewhere in the day. That is the whole answer. You do not need to hit it at every meal, and you certainly do not need to weigh anything to get close. Below is where those proportions come from, what each of the five food groups is actually for, and how to build the shape with ordinary meals. This is general information rather than personalised dietary advice.

## The proportions, from two independent sources

Two of the most widely used food guides in English say almost the same thing in different words.

Harvard's [Healthy Eating Plate](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-eating-plate/) is blunt about it: "make most of your meal vegetables and fruits, ½ of your plate", then "go for whole grains, ¼ of your plate" and "protein power, ¼ of your plate".

The UK's [NHS Eatwell Guide](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/the-eatwell-guide/) describes it as shares of the whole day instead of one plate, with fruit and vegetables making up "just over a third of the food we eat each day" and starchy foods likewise.

The World Health Organization adds a floor rather than a proportion: [at least 400 grams of fruits and vegetables per day](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet) for anyone over 10, along with free sugars under 10% of daily energy and salt under 5 grams.

Different countries, different phrasing, same plate. That agreement is the reason this shape is worth trusting more than any individual diet trend.

## The five groups, and what each one is doing there

Proportions tell you how much. Groups tell you what. afiet works with five, and shows them as colours that fill in during the day rather than as numbers.

**Vegetables.** The group almost everyone is short on. They bring fibre, water, potassium and volume, which is why a fist of them next to a heavy dish changes the meal more than any substitution.

**Fruit.** Sugar arrives with fibre and water attached here, which is a different proposition from the same sugar in a glass. Whole fruit over juice, most of the time.

**Protein foods.** Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, nuts. This group is what makes a meal hold you until the next one. A plate with no protein is the one you are hungry an hour after.

**Grains and starch.** Bread, rice, bulgur, pasta, potato. Energy for the day. Wholegrain versions where you can, because the fibre changes how the rest of the meal behaves.

**Dairy.** Milk, yogurt, cheese. Calcium and protein, and in many kitchens the easiest group to hit without thinking about it.

> A day where all five appear is almost always a decent day, whatever the calorie figure turns out to be.

## Building the shape without weighing

You can read the proportions with your hand instead of a scale. A palm of protein, one or two fists of vegetables, a cupped hand of grains, a thumb of added fat. That is a plate that lands close to the quarter-quarter-half shape without a single measurement.

Some plates in practice:

1. **Eggs, tomatoes, cheese, bread.** Protein, vegetables, dairy and grains in one breakfast. Add olives and the fat group is covered.
2. **Lentil soup, salad, a slice of bread.** Legume protein, vegetables, grains. Yogurt on the side finishes the fifth group.
3. **Grilled fish, bulgur pilaf, plenty of greens.** The textbook version, in three components.
4. **Pasta with vegetables and a little cheese.** Grains lead here, so the fix is a bigger salad rather than a smaller plate.

The detailed version of this, with gram equivalents and awkward mixed dishes, is in [hand portion sizes: measure your plate without a scale](/en/blog/hand-portion-sizes-without-a-scale).

## What "balanced" does not mean

It does not mean equal. Half the plate being vegetables is not balance in the arithmetic sense; it is balance in the sense that matters, which is that the groups you are usually short on get the space.

It does not mean perfect at every meal. The NHS is explicit that you should try "to get the balance right over a day or even a week" rather than at each meal. Breakfast can be bread and cheese. The day still has two more plates in it.

And it does not mean restriction. There is no group on the list called "forbidden". A dessert is not outside the system; it sits next to it, and the plate around it is what you actually control.

If the rules-and-guilt version of eating is what you are trying to leave behind, [why restrictive diets backfire](/en/blog/why-restrictive-diets-backfire) covers that loop directly.

## The group you are probably missing

If you audit a normal week, the gap is almost always vegetables, and it is usually not a knowledge gap. Nobody thinks vegetables are bad for them. They are simply the thing that requires washing, chopping and a pan when you are tired.

Practical fixes beat willpower here. Keep a vegetable that needs no preparation in reach: tomatoes, cucumber, carrots, a bag of greens. Add a fist of something to whatever you were already cooking rather than planning a separate vegetable dish. Soup counts. Frozen counts.

The point of seeing five colours instead of one number is exactly this: a total tells you nothing about which group is empty, and the empty group is the only actionable information in the whole day.
